Abstract
Book publishing firms in Nigeria continue to depend on analogue, relationship-driven methods to market to the schools, libraries, and bookstores on which industry revenues depend, even as institutional buyers digitize their procurement processes. The specific problem explored is that executives of Nigerian book publishing firms have not strategically integrated information technology into business-to-business marketing practices. The purpose of this qualitative multiple case study is to explore how publishing executives of book publishing firms in Nigeria perceive the role of information technology in shaping business-to-business marketing strategies and the challenges they experience when integrating information technology into those strategies. The study is framed by the technology-organization-environment framework, supplemented by relationship marketing theory and institutional theory. Evidence will be drawn from three triangulated sources across approximately six organizational cases: semi-structured interviews with 12 to 18 publishing executives, 24 to 36 firm-produced marketing documents, and 18 to 30 externally produced procurement and industry records, all analyzed through the six-phase thematic analysis procedure. The potential findings may inform publishers’ technology-investment decisions, strengthen business-to-business marketing practice in the industry, and support policy interventions that improve the distribution of educational materials in Nigeria.
Keywords: business-to-business marketing, information technology adoption, Nigerian book publishing, qualitative multiple case study, technology-organization-environment framework.

Chapter One: Introduction
Information technology (IT) has transformed the publishing business across the globe, especially in the field of business-to-business (B2B) marketing where personal, paper-based sales and distribution processes are being replaced by digital channels (Kalmykov, 2024; Söilen, 2024). However, the use of IT in publisher's B2B marketing in Nigeria has been inconsistent and superficial: while Nigeria has been improving mobile and internet infrastructure and has enacted some national digital policies, the majority of book publishers still rely on personal visits, telephone calls, and informal networks for accessing the institutions on which they rely for their revenues (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023c; International Trade Administration, 2024; Owolabi, 2024). The study focused on the role of IT in B2B marketing strategies and the problems of incorporating IT into B2B marketing strategies, the contrast between the global digital imperative, and local practice in marketing.
The background of the problem, the problem statement, the purpose of the study, the theoretical and conceptual framework, the research questions, the nature of the study, the definitions of the terms, the significance of the study, and a summary that previews the remainder of the chapters are presented in Chapter One to establish the foundation for the study.
Problem Background
The shallow application of IT in B2B marketing is the first challenge for the Nigerian book publishing industry. Even where digital tools have been adopted, publishers tend to use them informally for communication and general visibility rather than as a structured system of client management: social media substitutes for customer relationship management, and messaging applications substitute for structured procurement engagement (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023b, 2023c). In the print book market, evidence revealed that despite publishers' recognition of the commercial potential of digital innovation, their readiness for digital publishing remained relatively low (Ifeduba, 2020). The adverse effects of this superficiality are that publishers lose visibility and access in institutional procurement processes, institutions lose timely access to educational materials, and publishers lose sales pipelines, which in turn slows down or makes access to their materials less reliable for their target institutions (International Trade Administration, 2024; Owolabi, 2024).
The second is book piracy, which diminishes the value of publisher revenues, which would otherwise be spent on technology and market development. Nigeria's piracy has thrived in the face of poor enforcement, high costs of books, and poor technological protection of content (Ahmadu, 2017; Nwogu, 2014). The Copyright Act of 2022 was an update to the legal regime, however, there has been a lack of enforcement capabilities to address the vastness of infringement (Copyright Act, 2022; Uguru & Umobong, 2022). The negative effects are lost sales, lowered author royalties, and decreased investment ability, while the victims are publishers, authors and legitimate booksellers who lost out on the margins because of counterfeit value chain (Ahmadu, 2017; Owolabi, 2024).
A last issue is the lack of infrastructure that hampers digitally supported marketing, especially in nonurban areas. The following factors increase the operating cost of all digital efforts and deter continued investment in online marketing systems: unreliable electricity, poor connectivity, and high costs of data and equipment (Sadiq et al., 2022). Despite a general improvement in national indicators of internet access and mobile adoption, progress has been uneven, and even in rural areas, users continue to be significantly underserved (DataReportal, 2025; International Trade Administration, 2024). The negative consequence is an expanding inequity in the sector: publishers, schools, bookstores, and libraries in underserved areas are unable to participate in digital commerce, while the consumers they serve have limited access to educational resources (Sadiq et al., 2022).
The fourth is the digital skills deficit and financial problems within publishing companies. Access to affordable credit, the source of investment in technology and training, is limited in the small and medium enterprises (SMEs) of Nigeria that constitute the bulk of the publishing industry (Effiom & Edet, 2022; Owolabi, 2024). Meanwhile, a lack of digital skills and managerial experience with new technologies hinders the translation of experimentation into strategic adoption (Eze et al., 2020; Sadiq et al., 2022). The negative outcomes are stalled digital initiatives and inconsistent marketing practice across leadership and operational levels; those affected are the SME publishers, their employees whose skills stagnate, and the institutional clients who receive uneven service (Effiom & Edet, 2022; Eze et al., 2020).
Problem Statement
The specific problem being explored is that executives of Nigerian book publishing companies have not successfully applied IT in their B2B marketing strategies, continuing instead to rely on traditional analogue marketing approaches grounded in personal relationships with institutional buyers. The negative effect of this problem is that publishers lose visibility and standing within institutional procurement processes that are increasingly digitized, which slows and destabilizes the sales pipelines on which their revenues depend and constrains their ability to compete for the largest institutional contracts.
The cause of this problem goes back to the industry's overreliance on personal selling to schools, bookstores and libraries, which was adopted from the assessment-driven structure of the Nigerian book market, and has remained in place despite the digitalization of the industry's procurement and customer relationship (Kalmykov, 2024; Owolabi, 2024). Researches on the industry revealed low readiness in digital publishing, lack of big data and analytics practice, and the tendency to use digital channels for publicity instead of to manage digital B2B accounts (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023b; Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023c; Ifeduba, 2020). Significant because institutional buyers are increasingly going digital in their purchasing workflow, and publishers may be structurally excluded from industry's most lucrative market segments unless they can adapt to digital workflows (Ayawei et al., 2023; International Trade Administration, 2024). The issue is also of national development importance as an efficient publishing market for schools and libraries is lacking, reducing the availability of school educational materials in a nation with inequitable access to books (International Trade Administration, 2024; Owolabi, 2024).
Purpose of the Study
The purpose of this qualitative multiple case study is to explore how publishing executives of book publishing firms in Nigeria perceive the role of information technology in shaping business-to-business marketing strategies and the challenges they experience when integrating IT into those strategies. The overarching goal of the study is to generate context-based understanding that may guide technological, organizational, and environmental decisions related to IT use in B2B marketing within the Nigerian book publishing industry, potentially informing the strategic direction of publishing companies and policymakers.
Theoretical Framework
The key theoretical basis of this research is the technology-organization-environment (TOE) model of technological innovation developed by Tornatzky and Fleischer (1990). The main principle of the framework is that the use and adoption of technological innovation within a firm depend on three contexts that interact with one another: technological context (the characteristics and perceived advantages of existing technologies); organizational context (the size and leadership of the firm, its resources, and its internal communications); and environmental context (the structure of the industry, competitors, regulation, and market conditions) (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990). Recent peer-reviewed research has applied the TOE framework to B2B digital adoption questions similar to this study, including e-commerce adoption among Nigerian and South African SMEs (Ayawei et al., 2023) and digital leadership in developing-country enterprises (Barkley & Jokonya, 2024). The TOE framework aligns with the study problem because low strategic IT integration in Nigerian publishing appears to arise from precisely these interacting conditions, and it aligns with the multiple case study design because each publishing firm can be examined as a bounded case in which the three contexts operate together. The framework informed data collection by supplying the sensitizing domains of the interview and document review protocols, and it will inform data analysis by providing an organizing lens for interpreting themes within and across cases.
The study is supplemented by relationship marketing theory, introduced by Morgan and Hunt (1994) in their commitment-trust theory of relationship marketing. The theory's main tenets are that trust and relationship commitment are the central mediating variables of successful inter-firm exchange and that relationships characterized by trust and commitment produce cooperation, reduced conflict, and long-term orientation (Morgan & Hunt, 1994). Subsequent peer-reviewed research has applied the theory to comparable B2B and digital-channel contexts, showing that relationship marketing investments improve exchange outcomes through trust and commitment (Palmatier et al., 2006) and that marketing automation and social selling transform rather than displace trust-based relationship management (Ancillai et al., 2019). The theory is relevant to the study problem because B2B book marketing in Nigeria is conducted through long-term relationships with institutional book buyers, and any technology that mediates such relationships should not reduce or be detrimental to the level of trust and commitment within the relationship. The theory will guide data collection by informing interview questions on the effect of digital tools on client trust, and will guide data analysis by suggesting interpretations of themes relating to relationship quality.
The study is also enriched by institutional theory, as presented by Scott (1995). Scott (1995) argued that three institutional strands—the regulative, the normative, and the cultural-cognitive guide the form of organizational behavior. The theory's standing as an established lens is well documented: a cross-disciplinary review of 511 articles published in 210 peer-reviewed journals between 1978 and 2008 found institutional theory to be widely used across management, business, and sociology, while its application within information systems research remained comparatively limited (Weerakkody et al., 2009). Current peer-reviewed scholarship has applied institutional theory to technology-adoption questions in emerging economies, showing that institutional pressures help explain varying rates of adoption within the same market (Awa et al., 2015). The study problem is therefore consistent with institutional theory, which explains the pressures on publishers' technology adoption arising from the Nigerian Copyright Commission, industry associations such as the Nigerian Publishers Association, and professional norms. The theory will guide data collection by helping to formulate questions regarding regulatory, normative, and cultural factors that may affect adoption decisions, and will guide data analysis by helping to categorize the environmental pressures identified in the data.
Research Questions
This study was guided by two research questions, each of which was related to the problem, purpose, and qualitative multiple case study design.
Research Question 1: How do publishing executives of book publishing firms in Nigeria describe the role of information technology in their business-to-business marketing strategies?
Research Question 2: What challenges do publishing executives of book publishing firms in Nigeria experience when integrating information technology into business-to-business marketing strategies?
The first research question will address the study purpose directly by investigating executives' perceptions and descriptions of the role of IT in B2B marketing strategy. Previous studies have found that Nigeria has not embraced big data in publishing processes and that digital channels have been used primarily for visibility rather than for strategic account management by Nigerian publishing houses (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023b, 2023c). This question therefore addresses the technological context of the TOE framework—the tools available and how they are used, together with the organizational context in which those tools are embedded (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990).
The second research question will address the barriers that publishing executives experience when integrating IT into B2B marketing. Studies of Nigerian and comparable emerging-market firms have identified infrastructure deficits, financing constraints, skills shortages, and organizational resistance as recurring obstacles to digital marketing adoption (Ayawei et al., 2023; Sadiq et al., 2022; Tamirat & Zewdie, 2023). This question aligns with all three TOE contexts because integration challenges may be technological, such as tool cost and complexity; organizational, such as resource and skill limitations; and environmental, such as infrastructure, piracy, and regulation (Baker, 2012; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990).
Nature of the Study
This section describes the nature of the study. It presents the research methods and qualitative approach, the multiple case study research design, the data collection procedures for the study's three triangulated data sources, and the data analysis procedure, each of which is detailed below.
Research Methods
This study will employ a qualitative approach. Qualitative methodology is suitable when the purpose of the research is to explore executives' perceptions, meanings, and contextual explanations, rather than to measure variables or test hypotheses (Braun & Clarke, 2021; Hennink & Kaiser, 2022). Qualitative inquiry is appropriate when the intent is to produce detailed descriptions and credible explanations grounded in participants' experiences of organizational practice (Nowell et al., 2017; Tracy, 2010).
Research Design
The research design will be a multiple case study. A multiple case study is suitable because the phenomenon of interest—IT-enabled B2B marketing, exists within the real-world conditions of individual publishing firms, and because comparison across firms will allow patterns to be identified in the analysis while preserving the context of each single case (Priya, 2021; Rashid et al., 2019). Methodological guidance indicates that case study research requires a clear chain of evidence from data to interpretation, a detailed protocol, and transparent case selection (Rashid et al., 2019; Verleye, 2019). Participating publishing firms will form bounded cases based on their operation within the Nigerian book publishing market and their involvement in B2B marketing relationships with institutional buyers.
Data Collection
In line with triangulation, an attribute of a multiple case study, this study will use three data sources (Bowen, 2009; Rashid et al., 2019). The primary data will be collected using semi-structured interviews, the first data source. The target population will be senior executives of book publishing companies in Nigeria, such as executive directors, marketing directors, senior sales executives, and digital or IT directors. Individual semi-structured interviews of up to 45 minutes, conducted in person or by secure videoconference, are proposed as the data collection method. The proposed sampling strategy is purposeful maximum variation sampling to include a wide range of publishers, a range of publisher firm sizes, and a range of self-reported digital adoption levels (Palinkas et al., 2015). The proposed sample size is 12 to 18 executives across approximately six organizational cases, based on the saturation and information power principles (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022; Malterud et al., 2016; Saunders et al., 2018).
The second data source will be secondary data collected by reviewing organizational documents. The proposed population comprises publicly available and voluntarily provided marketing and corporate documents of the case companies, such as catalogs, websites, marketing materials, and descriptions of digital platforms. This study will use structured document review as a data collection method, guided by a document review protocol designed by the researcher (Bowen, 2009). The proposed sampling strategy is purposeful criterion sampling of documents that directly relate to B2B marketing practice and the use of technology in B2B contexts (Palinkas et al., 2015). The proposed sample size is four to six documents per case, for a total sample size of 24 to 36 documents.
Two distinct classes of archival document are used in preference to direct observation of B2B marketing operations. Firm-produced documents record how a publisher represents its own B2B marketing and technology use, whereas externally produced procurement and industry records evidence the digitizing institutional environment that the publisher must enter. Reading the two classes against each other, and against the interview accounts, allows any discrepancy between stated practice and market requirement to be examined directly. This design satisfies the triangulation requirement of the multiple case study using archival evidence that is either publicly available or voluntarily released, and it avoids the site-access and third-party consent difficulties that observation of live commercial negotiation would create (Bowen, 2009; Rashid et al., 2019).
Data Analysis
All three data sources will be analyzed using the six-phase thematic analysis procedure of Braun and Clarke (2006), supported by the reflexive refinements the authors later articulated (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Analysis will proceed within each case before cross-case synthesis, and NVivo software will support coding, memoing, and an audit trail (Nowell et al., 2017). Applying the same thematic procedure to interviews, firm-produced documents, and external procurement records will permit triangulation of themes across the three data sources (Bowen, 2009).
Scope of the Study
This study is bounded to executives of book publishing firms operating within Nigeria and to their perceptions and experiences of IT use in B2B marketing strategies. The study does not extend to consumer-facing (B2C) marketing, to publishing firms outside Nigeria, or to a technical evaluation of specific IT systems; instead, it focuses on the organizational and strategic experience of publishing executives. Data collection is limited to informants in senior roles with direct responsibility for B2B marketing, sales, or IT-enabled marketing decisions and to firm-produced documents and external procurement records connected to those roles and to the markets those roles serve. These boundaries were selected to maintain alignment between the problem, purpose, and research questions and to keep the study feasible within the doctoral timeframe.
Significance of the Study
This study may contribute to the scholarly field of technology adoption and B2B marketing in emerging economies. The evidence currently available on Nigeria is mostly descriptive institutional analysis or cross-sectional surveys, which are more effective for mapping adoption than for explaining the managerial reasoning behind it (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023c; Ayawei et al., 2023). The findings of the study may provide explanatory depth to the existing literature on the interaction of technology, organization, and environment in a specialized creative industry, and the results may also help researchers in publishing sectors in other African and emerging economies draw conclusions that could be transferable to their own investigations (Tamirat & Zewdie, 2023).
The study can also be a contribution to professional practice. The results could help Nigerian publishers, especially SMEs which make up the bulk of the industry, make informed decisions on technology investment based on the results of similar firms' executives on the actual returns of various technologies (International Trade Administration, 2024; Owolabi, 2024). Results for successful practices of IT-enabled B2B could contribute to publishers' efforts to increase institutional market access and improve their client relations, while other value chain members such as authors, booksellers and distributors would gain from a more digitally savvy base of publishers (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023b).
Finally, the study may hold significance for policy and society. Evidence on specific infrastructure, financing, and skills gaps could inform the interventions of government institutions such as the Nigerian Copyright Commission and the agencies responsible for national digital economy policy (International Trade Administration, 2024; Nwogu, 2014). To the extent that a more digitally capable publishing sector improves the distribution of educational materials, the findings may support literacy, education, and the wider national development agenda (Owolabi, 2024).
Definitions of the Terms
Book publishing industry in Nigeria. The book publishing industry in Nigeria is composed of all the companies and professionals involved in the making, production, marketing, promotion and distribution of books in Nigeria, which is dominated by small and medium enterprises with limited infrastructures and faced with challenges of book piracy and partial market (International Trade Administration, 2024; Owolabi, 2024).
Business-to-business (B2B) marketing strategies. B2B marketing strategies are the plans and activities through which publishing firms create, communicate, and deliver value to other organizations, such as schools, libraries, bookstores, and government buyers, in exchanges characterized by high transaction values, long sales cycles, and relationship dependence (Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Söilen, 2024).
Digital transformation. Digital transformation is the process through which organizations use digital technologies to change their value creation, business processes, and stakeholder relationships (Hanelt et al., 2021; Vial, 2019).
Information technology (IT). Information technology comprises the digital tools, systems, and platforms used to create, process, store, protect, and exchange business information, including customer relationship management systems, digital content and distribution platforms, data analytics, social media, and e-commerce solutions (Legner et al., 2017; Oliveira & Martins, 2011).
Institutional clients. Institutional clients are organizational buyers of books, including schools, universities, libraries, government procurement agencies, and corporate purchasers, whose acquisitions occur through formal procurement relationships rather than individual consumer purchases (Ayawei et al., 2023; International Trade Administration, 2024).
Technology-organization-environment (TOE) framework. The TOE framework is a theoretical framework holding that a firm's adoption and use of technological innovation is shaped by the interaction of its technological, organizational, and environmental contexts (Baker, 2012; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990).

Chapter Two: Literature Review
Technology is reshaping the operations and livelihoods of publishers and their relationships with their customers in the publishing sector, particularly in developed countries (Kalmykov, 2024). The purpose of this qualitative multiple case study is to explore how publishing executives of book publishing firms in Nigeria perceive the role of information technology in shaping business-to-business marketing strategies and the challenges they experience when integrating information technology into those strategies. Evidently, in Nigeria, many publishers are beginning to align with technology, with cloud-based tools now being used as a basic operational framework for successful B2B sales. What makes the adoption of information technology (IT) in publishing operations significant is that it enables industry stakeholders to gain competitive advantage, and thus implies that businesses can transact without barriers, manage complex supply chains, and deliver value through digital channels. In both cases, Legner et al. (2017) and Söilen (2024) found that in developed markets, end-to-end communications between publishers and institutional clients are now enabled through data analytics, relationship management systems, and online procurement portals integrated to form coherent sales ecosystems. The positive impact of information technology has been highlighted in the literature in the context of developed economies, but the literature has been silent about its implementation in environments like Nigeria.
Developed world publishers are enjoying substantial and measurable benefits from IT developers and digital platform providers, but the potential of these technologies for developing countries like Nigeria continues to be underutilized for publishers. Recent studies have also shown that the under-use of infrastructure resources is exacerbated by weaknesses in institutional structures, making it not only technically difficult to use, but structurally so. The technological and policy environment in which the Nigerian book publishing industry operates is unfavorable and this contrasts with the role it plays in educational development and cultural life. Industry assessments (International Trade Administration, 2024; Owolabi, 2024) noted that low investment in technology and inadequate infrastructure have become barriers to efficiency and competitiveness, leaving many publishers dependent on traditional sales and distribution methods that are increasingly out of step with how institutional buyers now operate. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to investigate how publishing executives understand the role of IT in their operations and to capture their experience of digital transformation or of its absence, within the B2B relationships that define the Nigerian publishing market.
Technology-based academic work has passed through at least three distinct phases. Early literature focused on the determinants of the spread and adoption of enterprise technology, technological beliefs and attitudes, and most technological eras were viewed as a bounded innovation and adoption as a one-time decision (Rogers, 2003; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990). The second wave, particularly between the 2000s and 2010s, of enterprise systems, e-business, and platform technology adoption, slowly began integrating organizational capabilities and environmental pressures into adoption models (Baker, 2012; Oliveira & Martins, 2011). The third phase, which has gained more and more ground in the last few years since the late 2010s, which sees digital technologies as a constantly evolving infrastructure that changes processes, business practices, inter-firm relations and even the structure of industries, and that makes the distinction between adoption and transformation obsolete (Kraus et al., 2022; Verhoef et al., 2021; Vial, 2019). The technology-organization-environment (TOE) model has been widely employed in all these stages, but lately, the TOE has been expanded to incorporate attitudes and trust, partner ecosystems, and organizational strategies, especially in cases where the outcomes of adoption are relational and process-oriented instead of performance-related (Awa et al., 2017).
Scope of Review, Search Boundaries, and Limitations
A multilevel search strategy was used to locate peer-reviewed literature for use and that served as a foundation for this literature review. The thematic elements of the research were the keywords selected, and were identified by keyword searches done on the major academic research databases (ProQuest, ScienceDirect, EBSCOhost, and SAGE Journals Online). Moreover, an additional research was made by searching Google Scholar and Google Books to extend the research scope. The initial scope of the search comprised theoretical and empirical evidence related to the TOE model and adoption models. Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) were used to refine search queries, filtering data within databases and search engines. All sources were assessed in terms of their relevance and contribution to the field, such that the combined literature as presented in the reference list is germane to the current study. Most sources were published between 2015 and 2025. Integration of older content serves the reader in terms of context and thematic foundation. Search terms included Information technology, B2B sales strategies, Nigerian publishing industry, Artificial Intelligence in sales, B2B sales innovations, Publishing industry in Nigeria, Digital transformation in publishing, Sales technology among others as they helped to identify relevant literature.
Peer-reviewed journals, books, internet sources, manuscripts, and these were included in the literature search. One of the principal limitations is the lack of equal representation of peer-reviewed evidence relating to the Nigerian publishing industry specifically in terms of documented B2B digital strategy implementation reported in deeply qualitative or longitudinal designs. In response, the review employs a three-way cross (a) publishing scholarship and indigenous authority focused on Nigeria, (b) peer-reviewed studies of digital sales in publishing that include Nigerian evidence where available, and (c) broader B2B sales and information systems adoption research that offers transferable frameworks while remaining sensitive to contextual differences (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023c; Ifeduba, 2020; Järvinen & Taiminen, 2016; Salo, 2017). This three-way analytical approach reveals variations in methods and contexts, which limits direct comparison across findings and places greater reliance on conceptual construction than simple data aggregation (Hanelt et al., 2021; Vial, 2019).
Consumer-focused sales (B2C) or technical aspects of IT systems will not be discussed in this review because the focus will be actual business-to-business (B2B) strategy and industry challenges. Kraus et al. (2022) demonstrated that while individual challenges such as the technological skills required for information technology adoption are well-documented in isolation, there is greater reliance on quantitative studies and theoretical models. While these models are important for identifying relationships, the models are limited in their ability to explain the 'why' behind the numbers, and the 'how' behind the strategic decision-making processes of publishing executives, as is observed in the Nigerian context (Eze et al., 2020). This methodological gap points to a significant gap, namely, a lack of better understanding of how technological, organizational, and environmental factors interact in practice within the unique institutional setting of Nigerian publishing, which this study seeks to address. This study therefore will offer theoretical and practical implications, providing valuable insights for numerous stakeholders within and across the Nigerian publishing industry; stakeholders include publishers, authors, booksellers, library professionals, the academic community, and the general public. This study can make a major contribution to the existing body of knowledge across multiple important academic disciplines.


Theoretical Framework
The study is based on the technology-organization-environment framework and also the relationship marketing theory and institutional theory. Each of these frameworks is defined and described, its origins are discussed, a review of how the framework has been used in previous studies in similar contexts is provided, its relationship with the study is discussed and finally the alternative frameworks that were considered and why they were not selected are summarized.
Technology-Organization-Environment Framework
The technology-organization-environment (TOE) framework is based on the work of Tornatzky and Fleischer (1990) who contended that the process of a firm's adoption and implementation of a technological innovation is determined by three interacting contexts: technology, organization and environment. Technological context is the internal and external technologies that are in place in the firm and their perceived relative advantage, compatibility, and complexity; organizational context is the characteristics of the firm that include size, slack resources, leadership, and internal communication in the firm; and environmental context is the structure of the industry, competitors, regulation, and market conditions (Baker, 2012; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990). The relevance of the TOE to the field is well documented as well: studies of the TOE in the field found that it is one of the lenses that is most widely used and empirically supported in the study of information technology adoption (Oliveira & Martins, 2011), and studies of the TOE after adoption showed that it was useful for explaining how far and how deep e-business is used in the firm beyond initial adoption (Zhu & Kraemer, 2005).
Previous studies used TOE successfully in situations very similar to this study. A TOE anchored model was used by Ayawei et al. (2023) to investigate the adoption of e-commerce among B2B SMEs in Nigeria and South Africa, with the results revealing that while some factors had greater weight in one country, others had greater weight in the other, thus showing the sensitivity of the model to country context. Awa et al. (2017) re-examined the framework in an emerging-economy context and called for enhanced uses of the three contexts as interrelated rather than checklists. Research undertaken by SMEs in developing countries added the constructs of digital leadership to TOE and validated the flexibility of the framework to take into consideration relevant issues for the modern world (Barkley & Jokonya, 2024). In this study, TOE integrates with problem because the interaction of the three aspects of technology, organization and environment appears to cause the problem of low strategic IT integration in Nigerian publishing; with multiple case study design because the three aspects of technology, organization, and environment can be observed together in each firm of the Nigerian publishing industry as bounded settings for the study; and it structures the interview protocol, and both document review protocols, and it supplies an organizing perspective for thematic interpretation.
Relationship Marketing Theory
In the context of this study, the theory of relationship marketing emerged from the work of Morgan and Hunt (1994), who emphasized that trust and relationship commitment are the two important mediating variables in successful firm to firm relational exchanges. In the presence of trust and commitment, exchange partners cooperate, do not consider attractive alternative opportunities, and interpret potentially threatening behavior as harmless, whereas in their absence, relationships turn into negotiations based on transactional bargains (Morgan & Hunt, 1994). Meta-analysis results also supported the theory's applicability to the field, as relationship marketing investments were found to have a positive impact on the objective and perceptual outcomes of the exchange by means of enhanced trust and commitment, among the strongest mediators (Palmatier et al., 2006). Theory of commitment-trust in inter-firm settings has also been tested and refined by subsequent scholarship (Brown et al., 2019) and research on digital B2B channels revealed that marketing automation and social selling do not challenge the theory but instead transform it (Ancillai et al., 2019; Järvinen & Taiminen, 2016; Salo, 2017).
Relationship marketing theory is relevant to this study because Nigerian book publishers' institutional customers are reached through long-term, trust-based relationships; questions concern not only the adoption of technology but also how the technology mediates trust and commitment in those relationships. The theory was used to design questions that guided data collection in interviews, and it will help inform data analysis by shaping the interpretation of themes relating to digital tools and client relationships, which may center around trust, commitment, and relational continuity.
Institutional Theory
Scott (1995) conceived institutional theory, which states that structural and behavioral changes in organizations are determined less by competition and the desire for efficiency, and more by the need for organizational legitimacy. More so, organizations are embedded in institutional environments that influence behavior in three ways: through the regulative pillar of rules, laws, and enforcement mechanisms; through the normative pillar of professional standards and social expectations; and through the cultural-cognitive pillar of shared beliefs and taken-for-granted assumptions (Scott, 1995). The relevance of the theory to technology-adoption studies in emerging economies is direct because integrative studies of technology adoption in institutional contexts have also examined institutional pressures to explain varying rates of adoption within the same market (Awa et al., 2015; Ayawei et al., 2023). 
The theory has also been used to show that industry associations operate as intermediating institutions that shape the diffusion of interorganizational technology (Weerakkody et al., 2009). This finding gives empirical warrant to treating the Nigerian Publishers Association as a normative-pillar actor in this study rather than as incidental context. Weerakkody et al. (2009) further concluded that institutional theory frequently needs to be combined with other theories and research models in order to account adequately for the empirical context under examination. That conclusion supports the multi-framework design adopted here, in which institutional theory supplies the external-pressure mechanisms that the environmental context of the technology-organization-environment framework leaves underspecified.
The Nigerian publishing environment reflects all three pillars: the regulative pillar operates through the Copyright Act of 2022 and the supervision of the Nigerian Copyright Commission; the normative pillar operates through industry associations and professional expectations; and the cultural-cognitive pillar operates through shared assumptions about how books are properly sold (Copyright Act, 2022; Nwogu, 2014; Uguru & Umobong, 2022). These pressures can be seen as influencing executive technology decisions both positively and negatively; they were used to inform data collection through questions regarding the regulatory, normative, and cultural pressures on executives when making technology decisions, and they will be used in the analysis to help categorize the types of environmental pressures identified in the data.
Integration of the Three Frameworks 
The frameworks are used in this study as different analytical tools. TOE provides an explanation for the decision logic of organizations when adopting technology, relationship marketing theory is able to explain why the outcomes of B2B marketing rely on trust and commitment, rather than solely on technology, and institutional theory enriches TOE by detailing the regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive channels through which external pressures act (Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Scott, 2014; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990). The technological and organizational contexts of TOE, together with the relational mechanisms of trust and commitment, inform the first research question on how executives describe the role of IT in B2B marketing strategy. All three TOE contexts, enriched by the institutional pillars, inform the second research question on the challenges of integration. Alternative Frameworks Considered
Three alternative frameworks were considered and not chosen. The technology acceptance model (TAM) originated with Davis (1989), whose core concepts are that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use predict an individual's acceptance of an information system. TAM has been foundational in adoption research, but it was developed to explain individual user acceptance rather than firm-level strategic adoption, and it offers little purchase on the environmental and institutional conditions that dominate the Nigerian publishing context; it was therefore not chosen (Davis, 1989; Oliveira & Martins, 2011).
The diffusion of innovations (DOI) theory originated with Rogers, first published in 1962, and its core concepts are that an innovation's relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability shape its rate of adoption across a population of adopters over time (Rogers, 2003). DOI illuminates innovation attributes and adopter categories, but its emphasis on diffusion across populations aligns less directly with this study's focus on the strategic reasoning of executives within bounded organizational cases, and its innovation-attribute constructs are already absorbed within the technological context of TOE; it was therefore not chosen (Oliveira & Martins, 2011; Rogers, 2003).
The resource-based view (RBV) originated with Barney (1991), whose core concepts are that firms achieve sustained competitive advantage through resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable. RBV usefully directs attention to internal capabilities, but it underweights the external institutional and infrastructural conditions that the problem background identified as central to Nigerian publishing, and its competitive-advantage logic suits performance testing better than the exploratory, perception-centered questions of this study; it was therefore not chosen (Barney, 1991).
Review of Relevant Literature
This review is organized thematically, beginning with the historical development of book publishing as a B2B relational industry, followed by its technological, organizational, and environmental context, a critical synthesis of that evidence, and the resulting gap in the literature that the current study addresses.
Historical Overview of Book Publishing as a B2B Relational Industry
The development of book publishing as a structured commercial and relational activity has deep historical roots, but for the purposes of this B2B-focused dissertation, the relevant history begins with the emergence of organized publishing in Nigeria during the colonial and post-colonial era (Adam, 2021; Shercliff, 2015). Historical accounts of the Nigerian book trade record that international houses, including Oxford University Press, Longman, and Heinemann, established Nigerian operations from the 1950s onward, entering an already-active indigenous publishing landscape and relying from the outset on multi-actor book chains built around business-to-business relationships among publishers, institutional distributors, and educational buyers rather than on retail sales to individual consumers (Shercliff, 2015). Shercliff (2015) further reported that this colonial-era foundation continues to shape the structure of Nigerian publishing today, with educational and institutional buyers still accounting for the large majority of sector activity. Building on this historical overview, recent scholarship frames publishing not as a single industrial activity but as a networked market in which stakeholders continually reposition themselves around production, distribution, and consumption relationships (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023a; Owolabi, 2024; Ponte et al., 2017).
Mapping the stakeholders in Nigerian publishing has consistently shown an industry characterized by interdependence between firms and institutions (Owolabi, 2024). Although the studies disagree about where the determining influence sits. Ihebuzor and Ihebuzor (2016) located it in the political economy of the firm, arguing that manuscript-acceptance and production decisions are driven as much by power, status, and commercial considerations among stakeholders as by professional editorial judgment. Biobaku (2011) located it instead in professional formation, linking the conflation of publishing and printing to training curricula that emphasize production skills over editorial and marketing competencies. Owolabi (2024) located it in market structure, treating inter-firm dependence as a consequence of constrained demand and post-pandemic supply disruption. These are not competing explanations so much as accounts operating at different levels of analysis, and read together they suggest that the weak boundary between publishing and printing is simultaneously an economic, educational, and structural phenomenon. The practical consequence is common to all three: the absence of a clear boundary pushes publishers into hybrid arrangements with printers and press-publishers, and the prolonged operating history of Onibonoje Publishers illustrates how such printing-dependent arrangements have constrained Nigerian publishing houses across several decades (Iwu-James, 2011). Because these hybrid partners directly determine unit cost, their efficiency, or lack of it, shapes the pricing and credit terms publishers can offer to distributors and institutional clients downstream (Ihebuzor & Ihebuzor, 2016; Owolabi, 2024). What remains unexamined across this literature is whether publishers experience these hybrid arrangements as a constraint to be managed strategically or simply as an unavoidable operating condition, a distinction that matters for how information technology might be deployed against it. 
Nigerian studies also project a market structure that leans toward institutional clients between 2015 and 2025. Afolabi and Zolkepli (2023a) noted that Nigerian book publishing focuses more on educational publishing, particularly primary and secondary school textbooks, while general-interest publishing receives less investment because it carries higher demand risk. Owolabi (2024) reinforced this pattern, reporting that a weak leisure reading culture constrains demand beyond the school market; where the two studies converge is in treating institutional demand, rather than individual consumer demand, as the more predictable and structurally central revenue base for Nigerian publishers, though Afolabi and Zolkepli (2023a) emphasize risk avoidance in general-interest titles while Owolabi (2024) emphasizes weak reading culture as the underlying driver. This shared institutional focus creates a sales strategy that is more about institutional selling than consumer selling: publishers compete on curriculum endorsements and bulk purchasing, and relationships with institutional buyers help predict demand and secure positions within school systems and book retail chains (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023a). The purchase decision typically rests with the institution or channel partner rather than the end user, whether a student or household, so publishers must maintain multiple B2B relationships simultaneously, including with curriculum actors, school owners, and distribution intermediaries.
Nigeria-based scholarship on the sales function offers a further point of agreement, though it is agreement reached across a substantial span of time. Writing at the start of the period, Okwilagwe (2001) identified sales and publicity as central concerns of Nigerian publishers, consistent with a channel-focused perspective in which publishers must persuade intermediaries and institutional clients as well as stimulate readers to purchase books. Two decades later, Shercliff (2015) and Owolabi (2024) described a market still organized around institutional adoption and bulk procurement rather than individual purchase, and Darko-Ampem (2004) reported the same dependence in the scholarly press sector. The persistence of this finding across four independent studies, different sub-sectors, and a twenty-year interval is itself the significant result: the channel-mediated character of Nigerian book sales appears durable rather than transitional. Where the accounts differ is in tone and prognosis. Okwilagwe (2001) and Darko-Ampem (2004) treated intermediary dependence largely as a structural given, whereas Shercliff (2015) and Owolabi (2024) framed it as a vulnerability that constrains growth and exposes publishers to demand shocks. Neither framing, however, examines how publishers actively work the channel — how they court institutional gatekeepers, sequence promotional effort, or use communication technology to hold intermediary attention. The empirical implication for this review is that sales within Nigerian publishing is an inter-firm relational activity rather than consumer messaging, and that the strategic conduct of that activity is largely undocumented.
The quality of inter-firm relationships becomes more consequential where Nigerian publishers operate under distribution and cash-flow constraints, and here the Nigerian and international literatures reinforce one another. Ifeduba (2020), surveying 109 Nigerian publishing firms, positioned digital publishing as a response to longstanding distribution problems and coordination costs among publishers, booksellers, and readers; Darko-Ampem (2004) had documented the same coordination failure a generation earlier in the form of absent bookstores and unreliable transport. Their agreement across two decades suggests that distribution weakness is chronic rather than cyclical. Where physical distribution is inadequate, publishers rely more heavily on intermediaries they can trust to reach customers, aggregate demand, and manage delivery, which places inter-firm trust at the center of commercial viability. Relationship marketing scholarship supports this emphasis but does not endorse it uncritically. Morgan and Hunt (1994) established trust and commitment as the central mediating variables of successful exchange, and Palmatier et al. (2006) confirmed through meta-analysis that relationship investment produces measurable performance gains, though they found relationship quality to be a far stronger predictor of objective performance than commitment alone. Brown et al. (2019) complicated the picture further, finding that while trust enhances commitment, commitment can in turn erode trust — a reciprocal dynamic that the original commitment-trust model did not anticipate. Taken together, this body of work agrees that relational governance substitutes for formal adjudication where the latter is costly, but disagrees about whether deepening commitment is unambiguously beneficial. That unresolved question has particular force in the Nigerian publishing context, where long-standing intermediary relationships may be simultaneously the sector's principal asset and a source of lock-in, and where no study has yet examined the trade-off directly.
The history of book distribution in Nigeria also reveals durable informal sales networks that constitute a B2B structure in their own right, and two independent studies converge on this point from opposite ends of the market. Adam (2021), in doctoral research on the Kano Book Market, documented multilevel relationships among publishers, printers, booksellers, and readers in which coordination rests on repeated exchange, reputation, and inherited norms rather than formal contract, particularly in the Islamic and Hausa-language segments where production, sponsorship, and retail are linked through enduring social networks. Shercliff (2015) reported a parallel phenomenon in the popular fiction market, finding that informal publishing networks account for a substantial share of book distribution. The two studies agree that informality is not marginal to Nigerian publishing but structurally central. They differ sharply, however, in how they evaluate it. Adam (2021) treated these networks as a functioning institutional achievement with its own logic and resilience, whereas Shercliff (2015) treated informality partly as a symptom of formal-sector failure. Olofinlua (2021) occupies a middle position, showing that digital platforms can formalize some of these relationships without displacing them. This disagreement matters for the present study because it bears directly on how information technology should be interpreted: if informal networks are an achievement, technology that bypasses them may destroy value, whereas if they are a symptom of dysfunction, technology that formalizes them may create it. What none of these studies provides is evidence on how publishing executives themselves understand this trade-off. This institutional and historical perspective nonetheless allows the review to treat distribution not as a logistics problem but as relational infrastructure that governs market access, credit cycles, and demand information.
A further evolution of B2B relationships in Nigeria's publishing sector is the digital transformation of the period between 2015 and 2025, which is reorganizing the intermediary landscape rather than eliminating it. Afolabi and Zolkepli (2023a) explained that digital technologies facilitate self-publishing and allow authors to bypass some traditional gatekeeper functions, but the shift is revealing new intermediaries and new groups, such as those between platform operators, payment solution providers, and digital co-publishers. Their argument also brought out the publishing chain extended into the digital space, which is now made up of technology providers and data experts, in addition to traditional actors (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023a). Olofinlua (2021) also explored the example of Okada books and connected the platform with the distribution of homegrown Nigerian literature made locally through publishing in e-book forms, suggesting a new channel relationship in which access, pricing, and revenue shares are negotiated by platform companies with publishers and authors. Within this framework, Nigerian publishing fits within a business-to-business platform economy model: content owners and service companies are connected through terms established by digital intermediaries, and competitive advantage depends in part on how well inter-firm trusts are managed (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023b; Olofinlua, 2021).
Data analytics and data practices have also been identified by the literature as growing relational assets of Nigerian publishers with direct implications for business-to-business competitiveness. Afolabi and Zolkepli (2023c) argued that big data analytics has the potential to facilitate publishing development by improving distribution planning, market targeting, and service choices, while at the same time emphasizing the difficulty of gaining capabilities and the structural constraints that Nigerian firms face when they attempt to implement data-informed practices. In business-to-business, analytics is a contextual asset that relies on channel information that distributors, sellers, and platforms provide. Those publishers that cannot negotiate access to data or to partner systems are limited in making predictions and positioning choices, which in turn affects confidence towards institutional clients who require timely availability and compatible supply (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023c). This current situation indicates that relational capability and information rights will become an increasingly influential determinant of Nigerian publishing performance in the future, particularly as procurement actors seek accountability and as digital delivery methods continue to grow.
The most recent Nigerian research in management supports the importance of relationship maintenance. Meta-analytic evidence linked relationship marketing investments, including customer loyalty, to organizational performance (Palmatier et al., 2006), and Nigerian publishing evidence associated strong client relationships with sales outcomes (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023b). In most cases, loyalty research is carried out on consumers, but their research deals with the business-to-business topic: the returning clients are not only considered as intermediaries and institutions but as individuals. In this case, loyalty can manifest itself in schools' repeated purchases, order books' standing orders, and supply contracts with distributors. The study thus validates the main hypothesis advanced in the literature review that publishing firms in Nigeria that regard continued relationship as a strategic resource are capable of assuring revenue stability, even when there is uncertainty in demand, provided they manage cost and distribution factors well (Ifeduba, 2020).
Lastly, the literature suggested that publishing relationships in Nigeria are not haphazard, and that competitive terms of publishing contracts and risk sharing shape the choice of publishing partners. Owolabi (2024) examined the competitive challenges of Nigerian publishing companies and connected them with structural constraints affecting operations and market outcomes. The Copyright Act of 2022 reset the legal environment for the digital exploitation and protection of works (Copyright Act, 2022). In a B2B analysis, the implication of these studies is that publishers should treat compliance, rights management, and anti-piracy strategy as legal relationships, because piracy and ineffective litigation affect earning power, predictable sales, and how publishers structure credit and distribution agreements with channel partners (Owolabi, 2024).
Synthesizing these studies, this literature converges on a consistent characterization of Nigerian publishing as a B2B relational industry in which value creation depends on coordinated inter-firm exchange and in which institutional and intermediary buying accounts for most revenue. The convergence is notable because it emerges from studies with very different designs: historical research (Adam, 2021; Darko-Ampem, 2004), interview-based sector analysis (Shercliff, 2015), survey research (Ahmadu, 2017; Ifeduba, 2020), platform case study (Olofinlua, 2021), and literature review (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023a, 2023b, 2023c). Two persistent disagreements nonetheless run through it. The first concerns informality, which is read as institutional achievement by Adam (2021) and as structural weakness by Shercliff (2015) and Owolabi (2024). The second concerns agency, with some studies treating Nigerian publishers as constrained by environment and others attributing outcomes to managerial choice. What the corpus lacks is equally clear: it is predominantly descriptive and sector-level, and few studies examine inter-firm constructs such as trust, reliance, capability, information sharing, and relational governance across the publishing, printing, distribution, and school networks. Work that integrates distribution history and platform intermediation with B2B relationship theory would therefore advance understanding of how Nigerian publishers build channels, negotiate with institutional clients, and manage digital relationships under policy and infrastructure constraints (Adam, 2021; Ifeduba, 2020; Olofinlua, 2021).
Technological Context and Strategic Use in Publishing
The technological environment in publishing refers to the availability, access, and proper use of digital tools that assist firms in achieving business objectives. In developed publishing markets, many publishers rely on advanced B2B technologies such as electronic data interchange (EDI) for automated ordering and customer relationship management (CRM) systems for client tracking (Sadiq et al., 2022). Although technological capability has been considered a predictive factor resulting in adoption in previous research, this assumption is being further questioned as part of thorough research. Oliveira and Martins (2011) emphasized technological and organizational conditions as core explanatory factors for adoption, although later digital transformation scholarship argued that adoption models must also account for managerial agency and interpretation (Hanelt et al., 2021). Such a divergence can be seen in the publishing case, where Barkley and Jokonya (2024) showed that managers with prior digital exposure engage new marketing technologies more readily. These two contradictory findings show that technology adoption in B2B publishing cannot be adequately explained by using capability-only models, which limits the explanatory power of structural models alone. This is a significant point for the Nigerian context: even where publishers have access to adequate digital tools, adoption does not automatically follow, suggesting that organizational and environmental factors moderate the relationship between technology availability and technology use.
The Nigerian publishing sector is currently characterized by a significant strategic implementation gap, where the widespread adoption of digital communication tools has not translated into data-driven sales with authority. While firms are knowledgeable in maintaining social engagement, Nigerian evidence identified an enduring pattern of shallow, publicity-oriented engagement in which social media served visibility rather than structured account management (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023b; Tamirat & Zewdie, 2023). Like other small and medium enterprises in the region, Nigerian publishers rely heavily on accessible two-way communication platforms, such as WhatsApp, email, and social media to manage key relationships with authors, educational institutions, and booksellers. As Afolabi and Zolkepli (2023c) noted, some publishers rely on search engine optimization to make their firms more visible online and use advanced data analytics to learn about customer behavior. Related sector research confirms that there is a largely untested pathway to development (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023c), which stems in part from a lack of knowledge and skills related to digital technology. As a result, technology that could improve sales efficiency and increase institutional sales ends up being used only for informal communications or publicity.
In contemporary publishing globally, institutional clients such as library professionals, school administrators, and procurement officers are beginning their search for book products online. In Nigeria, these searches are often through digital platforms that ensure compliance and knowledge. As an example, the Tertiary Education Trust Fund (TETFUND) has a list of registered vendors who must register digitally before they can supply books. Similarly, some State Agencies of Education use digital procurement portals in bulk purchasing of textbooks. Furthermore, Library Consortia, managed under the National Universities Commission (NUC), use integrated online systems for book purchasing. This indicates the presence of some digital directives within the Nigerian publishing industry. (International Trade Administration, 2024).  But the question is, many publishers still do not know how to strategically position themselves in these systems. This study, however, highlights a significant lacuna in the study of strategic application of information technology of publishers in Nigeria as a key instrument to build confidence of the institutions to participate and compete in a formalized digital procurement world. This does not mean that platforms are missing, but rather a lack of digital procurement literacy or lack of willingness to change their existing and entrenched system of platform contacts to standardized online communications.
Nigeria's weak national digital infrastructure is a key factor that adds to the challenges of the strategic use of information technology. Sadiq et al. (2022) noted that while the public has access to electricity and the internet, power outages, lack of connectivity, and high data charges continue to pose challenges for effective online operations. This infrastructure challenge makes it more difficult for publishers to run online operations and to invest in digital tools that demand continuous power and high-speed internet. The publishing industry in Nigeria remains in its early stages of digital adoption, and publishers continue to contend with occasional power failure, unreliable internet connections, and expensive data charges in maintaining their online publishing services (Sadiq et al., 2022). Beyond tools and infrastructure, it is important to understand why publishers are hesitant to rely on technology and to examine organizational conditions within the companies themselves. The focus therefore shifts to strategic use rather than technology access, a closely related topic discussed in the next section on organizational leadership.
Organizational Context: Leadership, Resources, and Cultural Disposition
The organizational environment of publishing firms focuses on internal factors that determine their capacity to transform digitally, particularly those related to leadership orientation, resource ownership, human resources, and cultural inclination toward change. Empirical research commonly characterizes the internal barriers of Nigeria's publishing sector as a lack of finances, a lack of digitally skilled staff, and leadership reluctance to adapt to new technologies (Effiom & Edet, 2022). These components are not independent of one another; rather, they influence each other and produce a systemic difficulty that limits meaningful technology use. One of the current problems confronting many Nigerian publishers is financial constraint, because publishers tend to operate on thin profit margins and irregular revenues. Tight budgets limit investment in online infrastructure and the ability to recruit critical staff who can manage online sales systems, analytics systems, or automated B2B tools (Effiom & Edet, 2022). Even where small digital investments occur, a lack of trained personnel leads to underuse of the tools, reinforcing the impression that technology investments are not a useful strategic tool (Eze et al., 2020). This creates a self-reinforcing effect in which poor returns discourage further investment, stalling incremental gains in digital capability across the sector.
Although firm state is often interpreted in terms of material resources (i.e., financial, knowledge, and top management support), these factors do not explain the differences in adoption rates across firms with similar constraints, leadership knowledge and organizational identity are major arbiters in the processing and channeling of resources (Warner & Wäger, 2019). Leaders do not merely maintain the environment; they are those who create interpretations of technologies, which determine strategic priorities and organizational behavior. Warner and Wäger (2019) also noted that leaders engage in sense-making where they analyze environmental signals to construct strategic responses. The fact that the Nigerian publishing industry sometimes displays a tendency towards resistance to the implementation of digital technologies is often connected to the creation of professional identities due to decades of print-based operations. Top management may interpret digital adoption as a threat to editorial independence, reputation management, or relational standards, and middle managers may interpret it as the advantage of breaking new ground and market entry.
Leadership attitude is also a factor of digital adoption. Many publishing executives, particularly those who grew up in the printing era, prefer personal communication with clients (Eze et al., 2020). Their view of online transactions is that they are risky and impersonal. Younger staff may be more open to technology although not always because of desire due to resistance or otherwise of leadership. Ifeduba (2020) also confirmed this generational gap by noting that it is slowing down innovation as older managers cling to their long-established personal relationships and not online platforms in the case of B2B communications. A further dimension worth considering is that, although internal weaknesses present major challenges in any firm and do not appear in any firm as strongly as another, research by Ayawei et al. (2023) suggested a more worrying element, the volatile business environment in Nigeria may be so dominant that even a strong internal state would not overcome it. This shifts the primary discussion from firm state to that of environmental survival and this research will analyze it through conducting interviews with key decision-makers within the Nigerian publishing market.
Environmental Context: Piracy, Market Pressure, and Industry Challenges
The issues of piracy, regulatory adjudication, and infrastructure reliability have also been characterized as challenges to digital adoption in Nigeria due to the environment (Owolabi, 2024; Sadiq et al., 2022). However, as institutional theory suggests, these factors may be influenced not just by material constraints alone but also by normative and cognitive pressures, which affect organizational behavior (Scott, 2014). An example of the framing of piracy that has been treated as financial loss (Effiom & Edet, 2022) and supply chain disruption (Owolabi, 2024). However, Ifeduba (2020) described piracy as a symbolic risk that weakens professional standing and trust of digital platforms. These contradictory interpretations suggest that environmental pressures are subjective and mediated by managerial perceptions.
The external business context is the most powerful systemic vulnerability threatening the institutional competitiveness of the publishing industry in Nigeria (Vrontis et al., 2025). An example is publishing companies, such as Accessible Publishers Limited, Nigeria that compete not only with other publishers and booksellers but with street hawkers of pirated textbooks in cities such as Ibadan, Lagos, and Onitsha, which creates a multilevel competitive environment and dilutes market prices. The world is also creating a push that drives publishers to adopt digitalization, yet the Nigerian environment is not favorable to such activities. According to the literature, book piracy is one of the greatest challenges in the book publishing business in Nigeria. It is a business issue that presents a character challenge to stakeholders in the book value chain. What scares many publishers from exploring digital platforms where books can be easily reproduced and distributed unauthorized and easily pirated is often facilitated by poverty, high book prices, and weak enforcement (Ahmadu, 2017; Owolabi, 2024).
The Nigerian Copyright Commission (NCC) that was previously said to protect publishers is being hampered by insufficient funds and outdated equipment. Research by Nwogu (2014) and Uguru and Umobong (2022) confirmed that the NCC has succeeded in music and film, but its campaign against digital book piracy has been poor. It has no ICT expertise and framework to effectively monitor/prosecute offenders. This observation is consistent with the contextual power argument, which indicates that external market forces can be powerful enough to nullify the effect of internal policy decisions. (Scott, 2014). In Nigeria, this would mean that a publisher investing in CRM software would be wasting their money when piracy is high and poor infrastructure provides no opportunity to use it.
Sadiq et al. (2022) further documented how poor infrastructure, unstable power supply, and slow internet connectivity reinforce a posture of technological caution among publishers, who are reluctant to make IT investments that require conditions the environment cannot reliably supply. This picture of constraint is important and well-documented. Yet it is also partial. Kalmykov (2024) offered a more nuanced reading, arguing that institutional gaps do not simply prevent adoption but reshape it, producing a hybridization in which digital tools are selectively and creatively deployed alongside traditional practices rather than replacing them; Olofinlua (2021) documented a comparable pattern in the Nigerian e-book market. This perspective challenges the default framing of Nigerian publishers as passive victims of environmental constraint and raises a more productive question: under what conditions do some publishers find ways to make digital tools work despite hostile environments, while others do not? That is precisely the kind of interpretive question that interviews with actual decision makers are best placed to answer. Rather than responding in a standardized way to such conditions, publishing executives interpret and negotiate institutional demands in ways that reflect organizational history, personal risk tolerance, and relational interests. This is why research capable of illuminating how institutional forces are understood and acted upon at the management level is both necessary and timely.
This study will therefore adopt a multiple-case study research approach to understand how publishers perceive and respond to these external pressures. It will focus on how and why their industry experiences institutional piracy, institutional failure, and infrastructure problems affect their management choices to adopt or reject certain technologies. This will help the study bridge the macro and micro level of the study, the institutional level and the level of managers of publishing firms in Nigeria. On this contextual basis, the next section provides a deeper critical analysis of each dimension of TOE, considering tensions between global models and Nigerian conditions, contradictions within the literature that deserves a qualitative multiple case study, and how these tensions and contradictions are negotiated in the literature.
Critical Synthesis of the Technological, Organizational, and Environmental Evidence
The existing body of literature on the application of information technology in B2B sales is an in-house conflict that is between the global technology models and the operational realities in the local market. On the other side, global researchers like Kalmykov (2024) and Söilen (2024) have taken an optimistic approach to it, where the use of the cloud and CRM systems becomes a regular part of the function in today's B2B enterprises. In this light, technology is a simple factor of competitive advantage that enables companies to breach market boundaries, even though they are conventional (Legner et al., 2017). But there is a major exception to this when the global models are imposed on the Nigerian publishing context. What has been reported from Nigeria is the contrary, as it is premature to assume the maturity of the local digital environment, which is characterized by poor infrastructure, and makes it difficult to establish coherent digital sales ecosystems (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023c; Sadiq et al., 2022). The result is a form of dissonance for the Nigerian publishing executive, for whom the international literature promises leaner supply chains while daily experience supplies interrupted power and unreliable connectivity. This is why it is appropriate to conduct extensive research and understand how these leaders understand IT practice in an environment where the infrastructure that underlies them is very fragile and has limited support for the application.
Data Analytics and Big Data add to this tension. Authors noted that the development of the book industry in Nigeria has moved to the next phase with Big Data in mind (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023c), but the analysis focused on the possible measurable outcomes of working with data. In contrast, Sadiq et al. (2022) pointed to the reality that data availability does not equate to strategic understanding. They argue that the bottleneck in B2B is the interpretive capacity of the organization. This means the problem is not the absence of technology, but a perceptual error at the leadership level where IT is viewed as a magic bullet that can ease sales, not a tool that requires the firm to restructure and become sophisticated.
Moreover, although scholarship on artificial intelligence in sales anticipated that AI-enabled tools would reduce uncertainty along the business-to-business sales funnel (Paschen et al., 2020; Syam & Sharma, 2018), the Nigerian evidence indicated that the reality is far less straightforward. The use of digital channels can contribute to the perception of risk by a manager in an environment where piracy is widespread and the law is lax. A publisher must go through the process of negotiating trust and thus, the move to divert to IT is not only a technical decision but one that involves intricate negotiations as Uguru and Umobong (2022) posited, especially in relation to the digital medium, which is the main medium of piracy. This leads to a marked gap in the literature: most literature deals with the adoption of IT, but little deals with the process of interpretation, by which a Nigerian publisher chooses which technologies are safe enough to trust in a low-protection environment.
In turn, the technological (T) component of the TOE model cannot be investigated outside of the organizational (O) perceptions of risk. This study adheres to a qualitative multiple case study model as it puts an accent on how technology is perceived by the decision makers who are required to choose and not what the technology does. The methodology will provide an opportunity to dive deep into how and why a certain strategic equivocality and flexibility can be seen as the peculiarity of the digital experience of the Nigerian publishing industry.
The tension existing in the operational capacity and the managerial perception is a significant characteristic of the organizational aspect of IT adoption on the publishing industry in Nigeria. Although the challenge of lacking a physical digital skill gap in the workforce is the most prevalent argument in the small business literature (Eze et al., 2020; Sadiq et al., 2022), a more critical synthesis of recent literature implies that the obstacle can be more psychological than technical in nature. Barkley and Jokonya (2024) did not argue in favor of the skill-gap but identifies the bottleneck in the executive tier. In this light, the digital transformation can be viewed as something viewed by the top management not only as a way to be more efficient, but also as a source of threat to the hierarchical structure of control and the well-known gatekeeping position within the publishing house. This brings a contradiction in the interpretation of organizational readiness. Sadiq et al. (2022) noted that the organizational culture does not change when many Nigerian SMEs invest in hardware and software (the T in TOE). This contributes to what may be described as technological stagnation wherein costly IT tools are not fully utilized due to the fact that the reality of the staff is still dictated by manual and paper-based mentality. The literature therefore creates a paradox: the tools may exist within the organization but lacks the interpretive inclination to incorporate it in the B2B marketing strategies.
Further, Legner et al. (2017) and Vrontis et al. (2025) reported that the organization structure required in the context of the digital change is customer-oriented. However, in the case of Nigerian B2B publishing business, it is perceived that there is a potential risk that digital openness would sabotage personal, trust-based networks, which secure institutional contracts in the past. This is a significant qualitative void: how do managers go about resolving the internal contradiction between the global need to go digital and the organizational need to maintain high touch personal relationships? This study will focus on exploring these managerial perceptions in order to transcend the measures of adoption to the subjective resistance that defines the organizational environment.
In the Nigerian publishing industry, the literature indicates a drastic difference in terms of the influence of the environmental constraints on IT adoption strategies. Some accounts characterized the Nigerian B2B environment as severely constrained by currency devaluation and infrastructural decay (Owolabi, 2024), while others documented publishers adapting through hybrid print and digital strategies (Kalmykov, 2024; Olofinlua, 2021). The latter reading implies a change of perspective: IT is not substituting for what have traditionally been face-to-face B2B interactions in Nigeria but is instead a mechanism deployed selectively to navigate disorder. This tension is further complicated by the role of leadership. Eze et al. (2020) posited that the primary barrier is a skill-gap among the workforce. However, this view is contested by Barkley and Jokonya (2024), who suggested that the bottleneck is not technical skill, but a cultural resistance from executive leadership who perceive digital transparency as a threat to their traditional gatekeeper status. In this case, the issue of contradiction is the answer to the question of whether the problem is a matter of capacity (what the staff may do) or the matter of the perception of the leaders (what the leaders may permit). Moreover, the debate on piracy portrays a big gap in the existing literature. Uguru and Umobong (2022) viewed piracy as a legal-technological failure, which should be improved by enhancing digital rights management (DRM). However, Afolabi and Zolkepli (2023c) posited that in a low trust cultural setting such as Nigeria, technological impositions such as DRM tend to scare away their legitimate business associates who feel threatened by these instruments instead of securing them. This illustrates a key lived reality of Nigerian publishers the same tools that are supposed to safeguard their intellectual property are also the ones that destroy their B2B relationships with their customers. The combination of these perspectives allows it to be seen that the value of information technology in B2B marketing cannot be quantified to sales volume or conversion rates. Rather, it is the managers and their perception of technology and its application in their business contexts that determine its success. According to Morgan and Hunt (1994), in unstable markets, B2B relations rely more on trust and reliability instead of immediate economic benefits and more on long-term relationships satisfaction. This poses a dilemma in the Nigerian publishing environment: executives are embracing digital technology as an indicator of their professionalism and global competence, but still rely on their long-established personal connections as a means of dealing with uncertainty and risk. The qualitative approach is therefore essential to ensure that this balance is explained in its actual use in practice, which is beyond the scope of the quantitative indicators.
Gap in the Literature
The general observation of the review of the previous studies is that Nigeria's publishing industry is at an important juncture of transition. Digital transformation is crucial for industry to stay relevant, but the industry has several barriers to digital transformation, both technological, organizational and environmental. The aim of this doctoral study is to answer these challenges and offers a new insight which is practical and specific to the context. It can therefore be seen that there are four major gaps in the current research literature.
Contextual Gap: Why Publishing Is Not Just Another SME
Most of the existing studies on digital technology adoption in Nigeria have been conducted on SMEs in general, not on the publishing industry specifically (Ayawei et al., 2023; Effiom & Edet, 2022). Others focus on digital sales or e-commerce in general without narrowing down to the book publishing sector (Söilen, 2024), which has created a contextual gap, because the Nigerian publishing industry operates differently from other types of SMEs. Its products are intellectual and cultural in nature, not merely physical goods. Its client base, which includes institutions such as schools, libraries, and government agencies, also requires relationship-based and formal selling rather than open consumer selling. Therefore, what applies to other SMEs cannot be directly applied to publishing. Research is also needed that will fully examine the ways in which Nigerian publishers use and experience digital tools in their business-to-business (B2B) relationships in their unique environment.
The Methodological Gap: The Limits of Surveys in Explaining “Why”
The second gap is on the methods that were employed by past researchers. Most of the studies have been based on quantitative surveys, which are effective to indicate general trends or correlations, including the association between finance and IT adoption, but they cannot provide a comprehensive explanation of why and how these two interrelations occur. As an example, one cannot respond to such a question using purely numerical data as to why certain publishers do not employ search engine optimization (SEO) despite the fact that it might make their companies more visible on the Internet, or why fear of piracy is a factor that makes them want to stay off digital platforms. These are some questions that need to be thoroughly humanized in-depth studies. Therefore, qualitative multiple case study research is necessary to involve the actual experience, perception, and the decision making of individuals who head publishing companies operating in Nigeria.
The Explanatory Gap: Untangling the TOE Interactions
The third problem is the illustration of how the various factors in the technology-organization-environment framework interact in actual life. In Nigerian publishing, TOE interactions matter because technology choices are moderated by piracy risk and enforcement perceptions (environment–technology interaction), while organizational readiness influences whether digital marketing communication translates into consistent partner service (organization–technology interaction) (Ifeduba, 2020). However, research on these relations is sparse in the literature, especially in contexts that are specific to the publishing industry, and the literature lacks a model-based explanation that includes TOE along with the relationship marketing mechanisms (trust, commitment, and information sharing) and institutional pillars (legitimacy, compliance) (Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Salo, 2017; Scott, 2014).
The majority of the studies focus only on these three contexts and on the problems of technology, organizational constraints, and environmental constraints separately. It is not known how these factors interweave. For instance, what impact does the fear of piracy (environmental factor) have on management's financial priorities (organizational factor), or dissuade the use of an e-commerce platform (technological factor)?
This is where the results of Ayawei et al. (2023) indicate that the internal organizational factors were not statistically significant in explaining IT adoption. It implies that external pressures might overwhelm internal readiness, which needs to be explored more deeply using qualitative multiple case study inquiry.
The Applied Knowledge Gap: From Diagnosis to Solution
Lastly, there is a clear gap in applied knowledge. Many times the problems which publishers are facing have been identified, such as cost, skills, infrastructure etc, but few studies have focused on what publishers are doing, and their success or failure, in overcoming these challenges. Little empirical work exists to demonstrate the use of specific IT tools in Nigerian publishers to enhance B2B marketing or to build institutional relationship. This implies that there is currently more a diagnosis than a cure in research. The research gap is the understanding about technology and how it can provide tangible return on business in Nigeria when so well aligned to local realities.
In the context of Nigerian publishing, there is evidence of readiness pockets and adoption pockets (Ifeduba, 2020), and a clear recognition of the strategic value of digital marketing communication tools (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023b), but little scholarly attention has focused on the steps involved in developing digital platforms that enable publishers to transition from partial adoption to fully integrated B2B systems that will facilitate institutional account management, partner coordination and piracy-aware distribution strategies. This practical gap highlights the importance to research, not only the factors, but also the governance practices, the relationship mechanisms and the sequences of integration which can facilitate the practical improvement, while taking into account the constraints of institutions.
These four gaps are interrelated in a direct way and deliberately with the two research questions which are guiding this study. The main drivers behind Research Question 1 – How executives in publishing describe the role of IT in defining their B2B marketing strategies? – are the contextual gap and the methodological gap. The existing scholarship has not specifically analyzed the Nigerian publishing industry or used the depth of a qualitative analysis to grasp the nature of IT related decisions, narration and experience of those who make them. We can know how low adoption rates are, but quantitative surveys can't tell us what it's like to make that decision in a market where you're facing generational tension, fragility of infrastructure, and piracy. Based on the explanatory gap and applied knowledge gap, the concept of challenges to integrate IT in B2B marketing strategies is raised as the second research question. But to explain why integration fails and in which combinations of technological, organizational and environmental pressure, it is precisely the kind of explanation afforded by case study inquiry with interpretive analysis that is needed. All four gaps, combined, merit the study, let alone its specific design, a qualitative, multiple case, and a lived strategic reality study of Nigerian publishing executives.
Contribution of the Current Study
This study seeks to address these related gaps with multiple case qualitative study research of the ways in which Nigerian publishing executives see, experience, and respond to digital transformation within their B2B operations. The study will not only identify the existing issues but also how and why. This study will also speak to the human experience and context of technology adoption by providing accounts of the real-life experience of the organizational decision-making process and for real-life insights that practitioners and employees can put to practical use.
How This Study Addresses the Gap
The design of the study responds to each of the four gaps in turn. The contextual gap is addressed by bounding the sample to book publishing firms rather than to SMEs generally, so that the intellectual and cultural character of the product and the formal, relationship-dependent character of institutional buying remain in view. The methodological gap is addressed by the qualitative multiple case study design, which elicits the reasoning behind decisions rather than the frequency of outcomes. The explanatory gap is addressed by an analytic strategy that codes technological, organizational, and environmental material and then examines the points at which the three intersect within a single case, rather than reporting them as separate categories. The applied knowledge gap is addressed by directing the interview protocol toward what firms have attempted, what resulted, and what they would do differently, so that the evidence base extends beyond diagnosis toward practice (Kallio et al., 2016; Rashid et al., 2019).
Exceptions and Emerging Successes: Evidence of Digital Adoption and Strategy Testing
Publishing-focused evidence indicates that digital sales communication tools are being actively used by publishers, with social media, websites, SEO, email sales, and content marketing appearing as recurring practices in publishing contexts, including Nigeria (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023b). Empirical evidence from the Nigerian book printing market also suggests that some publishers have produced digital titles and sent online promotion, and that a measurable state exists among relevant market participants (authors, schools, libraries, bookstores) in ways that could support broader digital publishing operations (Ifeduba, 2020).
However, there are still at least three areas of limited evidence for the success. One is the fact that most studies available focus on adoption measures (availability of digital titles, expressed use of social media) instead of the relational outcomes that would directly demonstrate the B2B strategic success (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023b; Ifeduba, 2020). Secondly, the literature provides more information about the use of tools than about the maturity of integration, and integration and the maturity of integration and the use of digital communication for CRM and order systems as well as with partners remains uncertain whether it results in long-term relationship performance (Järvinen & Taiminen, 2016; Vial, 2019). Third, there are very few well documented instances of Nigerian publishing companies transforming to become scalable in a digital B2B environment, with comprehensive reporting of outcome. With no such cases in evidence, there is a need for cautious interpretation, though pockets of adoption and experimentation are present, and documentation of peer-reviewed digital success stories over the long-term in the B2B context is comparatively light on the evidence base compared to the documentation of barriers and constraints (Hanelt et al., 2021; Ifeduba, 2020).
This does not mean that success is lacking in practice, it simply points to a lack of documentation in the literature, particularly in peer-reviewed publications that is largely dominated by cross-sectional surveys and relatively small in volume of longitudinal and comparative case studies, as published in Nigeria (Vial, 2019; Warner & Wäger, 2019).
Summary
This qualitative multiple case study on information technology and B2B marketing in the book publishing industry in Nigeria was guided by a review of literature presented in Chapter Two. Based on the historical review, it had been found that Nigerian book publishing business has been a relational industry between the publishers and the customers, as the relationship between book publishers and the schools and bookstores/ libraries was based on personal relationship, market norms that were inherited and a need assessing based demand (Adam, 2021; Okwilagwe, 2001). The industry's digital evolution reviewed revealed that digital technologies have started to restructure intermediation, self-publishing and marketing communication, while big data and analytics were hardly seen in publications operations (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023a, 2023b, 2023c; Olofinlua, 2021).
The TOE framework was identified as the main lens and the reasons why it has been selected over other lenses, such as TAM, DOI theory and RBV was explained by the theoretical framework section (Barney, 1991; Davis, 1989; Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Rogers, 2003; Scott, 2014; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990). The evidence then was synthesized in each of the three TOE contexts in the thematic review. In line with the technological dimension, the global digital transformation scholarship revealed strategic opportunity, and the Nigerian evidences represented that digital tools are superficially used for publicity. In the organizational context, financing constraints, digital skills shortage, leadership disposition were found to be important internal conditions in the organizational context (Effiom & Edet, 2022; Eze et al., 2020; Sadiq et al., 2022). In the environmental context, the systemic constraints of digital B2B strategy were identified as piracy, poor infrastructures and gaps in regulations (Ahmadu, 2017; Nwogu, 2014; Uguru & Umobong, 2022).
The gap analysis revealed that the literature contained survey and descriptive data but lacked case data to explain reasoning of publishing executives about IT in B2B marketing, and the interaction of the TOE conditions in Nigerian publishing had not been deeply studied (Afolabi & Zolkepli, 2023c & Ayawei et al., 2023). These gaps aligned directly with the study problem, purpose, and research questions established in Chapter One. Chapter Three describes the research method and design through which the study addresses these gaps, including the target population and sampling, ethical considerations, instrumentation, data collection, and data analysis procedures.

Chapter Three: Methodology
The purpose of this qualitative multiple case study is to explore how publishing executives of book publishing firms in Nigeria perceive the role of information technology (IT) in shaping business-to-business (B2B) marketing strategies and the challenges they experience when integrating information technology into those strategies. The purpose of this chapter is to present and defend the methodological decisions employed to carry out the study, and to show their alignment with the research questions and the study's goals. The chapter presents the research design, the target population and sampling for each of the study's three data sources, my positionality, ethical considerations, data collection and instrumentation, and data analysis. The chapter then records the assumptions, delimitations, and limitations of the research, details the strategies for establishing trustworthiness, and closes with a summary that transitions to the presentation of findings in Chapter Four.
Research Questions
Two research questions guided this study. Research Question 1 asked: How do publishing executives of book publishing firms in Nigeria describe the role of information technology in shaping business-to-business marketing strategies? Research Question 2 asked: What challenges do publishing executives of book publishing firms in Nigeria experience when integrating information technology into business-to-business marketing strategies? Because the study is qualitative, no hypotheses were formulated or tested; the focus was on processes, meanings, and the contextual conditions that shape organizational behavior (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022; Tracy, 2010).
Research Method and Design
The research design will be a multiple case study. A multiple case study will be used because the phenomenon under study is grounded in real-life organizational settings, allowing depth within each case and cross-case synthesis of patterns across cases (Priya, 2021; Rashid et al., 2019). The methodological guidance for this study focuses on three key aspects: clearly explained case selection, a detailed protocol for data collection, and a clear line of analysis drawn from evidence to interpretation (Rashid et al., 2019; Verleye, 2019). All participating publishing companies will be cases in their own right, each operating within the Nigerian book publishing market and marketing book products to institutional buyers, intermediaries, and book distribution partners. To support triangulation of the evidence base, three data sources will be used: semi-structured interviews, firm-produced organizational documents, and externally produced procurement and industry records, as detailed below (Bowen, 2009; Rashid et al., 2019).
The approach in this study will be qualitative. I selected a qualitative approach because the questions I am asking of the organizations involved in this research require deep insight into organizational practice, decision rationale, and contextual constraints that could not be adequately captured through variable-based measurement (Braun & Clarke, 2021; Tracy, 2010). Qualitative inquiry is suitable when practitioners' perceptions and practices pertaining to strategy are to be investigated, and when detailed description and credible explanation grounded in practitioners' experience are required (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022; Nowell et al., 2017). The study will not use surveys, questionnaires, or other variable-measurement instruments to collect data; a brief participant information sheet will be collected at the beginning of each interview for administrative purposes only, containing contextual descriptors (e.g., role, years of experience, organizational function) used for case description and transferability rather than construct measurement, and the study will not test hypotheses.
Target Population and Sampling
This study will use three data sources. For each source, the population, participants or materials, inclusion criteria, sampling strategy, and proposed sample size are identified below. The section then addresses the logic of case selection; the researcher's positionality is addressed in a later section of this chapter.
The first data source will be senior executives of book publishing companies in Nigeria, such as executive directors, marketing directors, senior book sales executives, and digital or IT directors, who will have direct responsibility for B2B marketing strategy and for IT-enabled marketing and sales. To be included in the study, a participant must be an adult, must work for a book publishing company in Nigeria, and must occupy a senior leadership or management role with direct involvement in at least one of the following areas: B2B marketing strategy, sales strategy, customer relationship management, digital marketing, data use, or IT-enabled marketing processes. These criteria are necessary because only respondents with such responsibilities can give accounts that bear directly on the research questions. The sampling strategy will be purposeful sampling, which selects participants who have direct experience of the phenomenon and who are information-rich with respect to it (Palinkas et al., 2015). The planned sample will comprise 12 to 18 executives, with approximately two to three executives in each of approximately six organizational cases. This size is justified from the literature on saturation and information power rather than by statistical calculation: systematic evidence indicates that well-specified qualitative studies commonly achieve saturation within this range and that further interviews yield meaning saturation (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022; Hennink et al., 2017; Malterud et al., 2016; Saunders et al., 2018). The range is deliberately flexible so as to allow modest expansion should thematic development require it.
The second data source will be firm-produced organizational documents of the participating case firms that are publicly available or voluntarily submitted, such as catalogs, corporate websites, marketing communications, sales and distribution materials, descriptions of digital platforms, and procurement-related documents relevant to B2B marketing and technology use that participants identify as relevant and nonconfidential. Each document must originate from, or formally describe, a case firm; must relate to B2B marketing practice or to the use of marketing technology; and must be publicly available or volunteered without confidentiality objection. Documents that do not meet these criteria will be excluded. The sampling strategy will be purposeful criterion sampling, selecting the documents most directly relevant to the research questions within each case (Bowen, 2009; Palinkas et al., 2015). The proposed sample size is four to six documents per case, or approximately 24 to 36 documents in total, which is justified as sufficient to corroborate and contextualize interview accounts within each case while remaining feasible to analyze systematically (Bowen, 2009).
The third data source will be externally produced procurement and industry records that refer to, or govern, the institutional market the case firms serve. The population comprises published tender notices and vendor registration requirements issued by institutional buyers, supplier and procurement listings maintained by educational agencies and library consortia, and industry association bulletins and sector reports documenting market and regulatory conditions. Each record must be in the public domain or otherwise lawfully obtainable without breach of confidence; must postdate 2015 so that it reflects the contemporary procurement environment; and must bear on the segment of the institutional market in which the relevant case firm competes. Records that identify individual buyers or that contain commercially confidential pricing will be excluded or redacted at the point of collection. The sampling strategy will be purposeful criterion sampling of records that evidence the digitization of the procurement environment each case firm must enter (Bowen, 2009; Palinkas et al., 2015). The proposed sample size is three to five records per case, or approximately 18 to 30 records in total, which is justified as sufficient to establish the external procurement conditions against which firm-produced claims and interview accounts can be tested.
Two classes of archival document are used in preference to nonparticipant observation of live B2B marketing operations. The two classes are analytically distinct: firm-produced documents evidence how a publisher represents its own B2B marketing and technology use, whereas externally produced records evidence the digitizing institutional environment the publisher must enter. Reading each class against the other, and both against the interview accounts, permits any gap between stated practice and market requirement to be examined directly, which is the analytic purpose observation would otherwise have served. This design satisfies the triangulation requirement of the multiple case study while relying on evidence that is either publicly available or voluntarily released, and it removes the site-access, scheduling, and third-party consent risks that observation of commercially sensitive negotiation would introduce at the Institutional Review Board stage (Bowen, 2009; Rashid et al., 2019).
Cases will be selected using a maximum variation purposive logic (Palinkas et al., 2015). To establish diversity, organizations will be selected deliberately across dimensions that theory identifies as critical to the adoption of digital tools and to B2B marketing outcomes in Nigerian publishing: publishing segment, spanning educational, academic, and general-interest publishing, because institutional profile and procurement context vary across these segments; organizational size, spanning small, medium, and larger firms, because resource availability influences the capacity to adopt digital tools; and level of digital adoption, spanning firms that depend mainly on traditional sales methods, firms using digital marketing tools and customer relationship management systems, and firms using digital distribution platforms, because the level of adoption influences the capacity to extend B2B marketing activity. This variation strategy is intended to generate a broad evidential base across contrasting organizational conditions and to enable analytical generalization about how technological, organizational, and environmental factors operate under different circumstances (Rashid et al., 2019). Organizational access will be sought through professional networks and formal industry pathways, including sector associations where appropriate; where recruitment occurs through an association channel, permission will be sought to distribute an invitation notice through the association's communication mechanisms.
Ethical Considerations
The mechanisms of ethics compliance in the study include the following: institutional review, informed consent, protection of confidentiality, risk reduction, and data management. No human subjects can be recruited for research or data collection nor will I collect data without the IRB approval and completion of all Human Subjects training requirements. All participants will give written informed consent before taking part. Written site permission will be obtained from each participating publishing organization before recruitment within that organization, confirming that the organization permits members of its staff to be invited to participate, that interviews may be conducted on its premises or during its time where appropriate, that any firm-produced documents released for the study are released voluntarily and without confidentiality objection, and that the identity of the organization will be protected through case codes and pseudonyms. The consent process will explain the purpose and nature of the study, the anticipated time commitment, how the study will be recorded, potential risks/benefits, confidentiality measures, and the voluntary nature of participation and the ability to withdraw at any time without penalty.
Confidentiality is stressed and achieved by using pseudonyms for participants, case codes for organizations, and by omitting identifying information from transcripts, documents, field notes, and reports, because participants may discuss commercially sensitive practices. The key for re-identifying the data is not stored with the de-identified data and is available only to me. All digital materials will be kept in an encrypted, password-protected system accessible only to me, and all physical materials will be kept in a locked cabinet. In compliance with doctoral record-keeping requirements, all data will be retained for a minimum of 3 years after completion of the degree and then destroyed through secure deletion of electronic files and shredding of paper records. The study poses minimal risk; the primary risk is that a participant may feel uncomfortable disclosing organizational limitations or may feel exposed to negative consequences from discussing the study with colleagues or the public. This risk is addressed through the confidentiality of the study, the ability to decline any question, and thematic, de-identified reporting. To minimize the risk of coercion, I will not recruit individuals over whom I hold a formal position of authority that could be construed as affecting their professional standing, and gatekeepers will be used only to permit access, not to monitor participation decisions. Recruitment communications will explicitly state that participation is voluntary, that refusal carries no consequence, and that I am acting in an academic capacity.
Instrumentation
Because this is a multiple case study with three data sources, three instruments have been developed: a semi-structured interview protocol, a firm-produced marketing document review protocol, and an external procurement and industry record review protocol. Each instrument appears in the appendices, and this section describes how each was developed and how its trustworthiness will be established.
The first instrument is the semi-structured interview protocol (Appendix A). I developed the protocol directly from the research questions and from the sensitizing domains of the study's frameworks: the technology-organization-environment framework guided systematic coverage of technological, organizational, and environmental factors in IT adoption and use, and relationship marketing theory justified questions on how trust and commitment operate within technology-mediated B2B relationships (Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Oliveira & Martins, 2011; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990). Semi-structured interviewing is appropriate when the researcher seeks participants' perspectives while maintaining consistent coverage of the domains related to the research questions, and systematic development of the interview guide strengthens the rigor of qualitative data collection (Kallio et al., 2016). I establish the trustworthiness of this instrument through the framework-anchored development process, through expert review by at least one qualitative methodology peer reviewer, and through pilot testing with a minimum of two and a maximum of three individuals who meet the eligibility criteria but will not join the main sample; each pilot interview will be conducted with the full protocol, audio-recorded with consent, and reviewed for question clarity, logical flow, thematic completeness relative to the research questions, and appropriateness of length and probe depth, with all revisions recorded in the study audit trail (Kallio et al., 2016; Nowell et al., 2017).
The second instrument is the firm-produced marketing document review protocol (Appendix B). The protocol was created from published guidance on document analysis, which recommends the systematic examination of organizational documents as a means of supporting and complementing interview data by supplying context, corroboration, and complementary evidence (Bowen, 2009). The protocol captures source, document type, date, intended audience, B2B marketing references, information technology references, and analytic notes keyed to the framework domains of the study. The trustworthiness of this instrument rests on the published document analysis procedure (Bowen, 2009), on peer review of the protocol prior to use, and on an audit trail recording how each document was identified, screened for inclusion, and coded (Nowell et al., 2017).
The third instrument is the external procurement and industry record review protocol (Appendix C). It was developed from the same document analysis guidance but is directed at externally produced evidence rather than at self-representation by the firm (Bowen, 2009). The protocol records the issuing body, record type, date, jurisdiction or market segment, the digital or analogue mechanism through which suppliers must engage, the requirements imposed on suppliers, and analytic notes on the institutional pillar to which the requirement corresponds. Because the analytic value of this source lies in comparison, the protocol separates description of the requirement from assessment of whether the corresponding case firm evidences the capability to meet it, so that inference is not made at the point of recording. Trustworthiness will be established through peer review of the protocol prior to use, through provenance recording for every record collected, and through audit trail entries for each screening and coding decision (Bowen, 2009; Nowell et al., 2017).
No third party instrument that requires copyright or intellectual property permission is used in this study; all three instruments were developed by the researcher specifically for this study and the finalized protocols are found in Appendix A, B and C and will be submitted with the Institutional Review Board application.
Data Collection
Data collection is systematic to avoid any ethical issues, applied in a uniform way across cases, and is followed up on each of the three data sources. Across all sources, collection begins only after IRB approval and site permissions are secured, and all materials are de-identified promptly using pseudonyms and case codes.
For the first data source, I will recruit interview participants through professional and organizational contact channels suited to the Nigerian publishing environment, drawn from the population of senior publishing executives described above, screened against the inclusion criteria, and selected through purposeful maximum variation sampling toward the proposed sample of 12 to 18 executives across approximately six cases (Palinkas et al., 2015). Interviews of up to 45 minutes will be scheduled at times and in settings that favor privacy, either face-to-face in professional settings or through a secure videoconferencing platform. At the start of every interview I will review the consent form, reaffirm permission to record, and restate participants' rights, including the right to discontinue. With permission, interviews will be audio-recorded; if a participant declines recording, I will rely on detailed contemporaneous notes while following the same protocol. After each interview I will prepare field notes capturing contextual observations and analytic reflections. Audio files will be transcribed word for word within 48 hours of each interview, transcripts will be checked against the recordings, and de-identification will be performed immediately.
For the second data source, I will collect organizational documents that meet the inclusion criteria, either from public sources or as voluntarily provided by the case firms, screened through criterion sampling toward the proposed four to six documents per case (Bowen, 2009; Palinkas et al., 2015). Every document will be entered into the document review protocol, assigned to its case code, and kept in an encrypted study archive; documents will be accepted only when there is no confidentiality objection that is contrary to the study's de-identification commitments.
For the third data source, externally produced procurement and industry records will be retrieved from institutional and public repositories, from the published notices of institutional buyers, and from industry association and sector publications, and will be screened through criterion sampling toward the proposed three to five records per case (Bowen, 2009; Palinkas et al., 2015). For each record, the issuing body, date of issue, means of retrieval, and date of retrieval will be logged before analysis, so that provenance can be verified independently. Records will be matched to case codes by market segment rather than by named buyer, and any identifying detail concerning individual procurement officers will be removed at the point of entry into the archive. Across all three sources, emerging themes and codes will be reviewed to determine whether the evidence is becoming sufficiently rich to answer the research questions, rather than continuing to sample until a predetermined volume target has been reached (Hennink et al., 2017; Saunders et al., 2018).
Data Analysis
As is typical of multiple case studies research, I will employ the same method of data analysis, thematic analysis, for all three types of data in cases and then across cases. An appropriate and systematic way to identify, code and explain patterned meaning in qualitative data, themed analysis can be used in a reflexive and transparent way for rigorous interpretation of data (Braun & Clarke, 2006; Nowell et al., 2017). The six phases of Braun and Clarke (2006) will be followed: familiarization with the data, generation of initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report. The reflexive refinements the authors later articulated will also be adopted (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Interview transcripts, firm-produced document review records, and external procurement record review records will all pass through the same six phases, which will allow themes to be triangulated across the three sources within each case (Bowen, 2009).
All transcripts, documents, and field notes will be stored in NVivo software, which facilitates code organization, memoing, and traceability. The coding process will be inductive, covering the entire dataset, and it will be theoretically informed by technological, organizational, and environmental factors without limiting the data to a pre-existing classification (Braun & Clarke, 2021; Oliveira & Martins, 2011). Each organization will receive a within-case analysis that includes a narrative account of how the organization used, or did not use, IT to develop its B2B marketing strategy and what constraints it faced in doing so. Themes will then be compared across cases to note convergences and significant differences, and analytic claims will be supported through replication logic and contextual explanation (Rashid et al., 2019). The procedures will be transparent, including an audit trail of coding decisions, theme development, revisions, and rationales for analysis, and peer debriefing will be used to test interpretive coherence during theme refinement (Nowell et al., 2017). This chapter does not contain findings; they are presented in Chapter Four.
Assumptions
Statements accepted as true when designing and interpreting this study are called assumptions, and the following assumptions are justified below. First, it is assumed that participants would offer honest and considered comments about organizational practices. This assumption is made because the interviews served as the main source of information for this study, and the short-term confidentiality provided in this study is expected to reduce socially desirable responding when the topic is sensitive to organizational issues, as has been shown in prior research (Krumpal, 2013). Second, it is assumed that the purposeful sampling strategy would provide information-rich participants whose role-based perspectives would answer the research questions; this assumption is necessary because a small qualitative sample can only address the research questions if its members possess relevant experiences (Palinkas et al., 2015), and because the study's inclusion criteria limit participation to executives who have direct B2B marketing and IT responsibility. Third, it is assumed that the systematically developed instruments would facilitate credible and consistent data collection across the cases; this assumption is made because cross-case comparison assumes the domain is sufficiently covered across cases, and because the instruments were developed from published methodological guidance, peer reviewed, and pilot tested prior to use (Bowen, 2009; Kallio et al., 2016).
Delimitations
The boundaries that I imposed on my study are called delimitations, and each one represents a conscious design choice. The study is delimited to book publishing firms within Nigeria and not other creative firms, because the study problem and the literature are focused specifically on the publishing sector, which helps maintain the context and case-study depth of the research. It is limited to B2B marketing strategy because institutional buyers form the revenue base of the problem set. It is not a technical assessment of IT systems but instead focuses on the organizational experience of using IT, as the research questions are directed toward executives' perceptions and strategic reasoning. Finally, it is restricted to informants whose roles involve the strategic and operational use of IT in marketing and sales, because only these informants can provide evidence directly relevant to the research questions. These boundaries were selected to make the study feasible within the doctoral timeframe and to ensure consistency among the problem, purpose, and research questions.
Limitations
Limitations are constraints that are not within my control and that arise from methodological decisions; they can affect interpretation, and I specify below what I will do to minimize their impact. First, recall and self-report will form a major part of the evidence base; I will counter this through triangulation of interview accounts with firm-produced documents and external procurement records, and by probing for the detail of what occurred rather than the impression subsequently formed (Bowen, 2009). Second, participation bias may occur depending on organizational willingness to participate; I will account for this limitation by implementing maximum variation case selection, drawing on multiple sources of recruitment, and reporting the characteristics of participating cases transparently so readers can evaluate the evidence base (Palinkas et al., 2015). Third, the results of this qualitative case study are not statistically generalizable; transfer to other settings will require readers to make judgments about contextual similarity, and to aid these transferability judgments I will provide thick description of the organizational contexts, participant roles, and market conditions (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Tracy, 2010). Fourth, because I am a former industry leader, I may hold a particular point of view on how to interpret the findings, but I will counteract this limitation using reflexive journaling, an audit trail, and peer debriefing procedures, as described in the Positionality and Trustworthiness sections (Berger, 2015). Fifth, the replacement of direct observation with a second class of archival evidence means that enacted practice will not be observed at first hand; this will be mitigated by requiring interview accounts of specific recent episodes and by testing those accounts against the documentary record from both classes of source (Bowen, 2009).
Trustworthiness
In this study the four traditional qualitative research criteria, credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability, as described by Lincoln and Guba (1985) and Nowell et al. (2017), are presented as indicators of trustworthiness.
Credibility is defined as trust in the truthfulness of the results that reflect participants' realities. In order to gain credibility, I will align interview questions to research questions with care, follow a systematic process for development and piloting of instruments, evidence will be triangulated across interviews, firm-produced documents, and external procurement records, and selected participants will be involved in validating interpretive summaries, on the understanding that qualitative knowledge is co-constructed rather than mechanically confirmed (Birt et al., 2016; Kallio et al., 2016).
Transferability: How much can be transferred to other contexts. For the purpose of transferability, I will include thick description of the organizational context, the role of the participants, the conditions of the markets, and characteristics of the case so that the reader can determine the relevance of the results to his or her own context (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Tracy, 2010).
Dependability: The stability and traceability of research process. I will keep a documented audit trail of methodological and analytic decisions, protocol changes and coding development to examine the trajectory from data to findings to ensure dependability (Nowell et al., 2017).
Confirmability is about basing conclusions in the data and not in the researcher's assumptions. I will employ reflexive journaling and memoing, record explicit evidence for each interpretation, look for disconfirming elements and negative cases during coding and theme refinement, and create questions that will encourage participants to discuss both the successes and the limitations, thereby minimizing socially desirable responding (Berger, 2015; Krumpal, 2013).
Researcher Positionality
Positionality is a critical methodological issue because it influences who has access, who connects with whom, how data are interpreted, and what ethical risks exist. I have leadership experience and professional visibility in the Nigerian publishing industry. This background may help me gain entry to trusted professional networks, enhance credibility with prospective participants, and support a detailed understanding of the industry's terminology, institutional relationships, and market structure. This insider role carries methodological advantages but also the potential for pre-existing assumptions to bias data analysis. I will address this risk by keeping a systematic reflexive journal throughout data collection and analysis. I will note any pre-existing assumptions and expectations about the organization or participant role before each interview and will reexamine these before coding, constructing themes, and interpreting findings. All analytical decisions will be recorded in the audit trail, and any changes to initial interpretations will be documented. These reflexive practices are designed to ensure that my industry background contributes contextual sensitivity rather than reducing interpretive openness (Berger, 2015; Tracy, 2010).
Summary
This chapter outlined the methodology adopted in the study to explore the perception of publishing executives of book publishing firms of Nigeria on the role of information technology in the book publishing industry's B2B marketing strategies and the problems associated to the integration of information technology in the B2B marketing strategies in the book publishing industry in Nigeria. The chapter restated the two research questions and justified the qualitative methodology and the multiple case study design with recent peer-reviewed methodological literature (Priya, 2021; Rashid et al., 2019). It identified the three triangulated data sources of the study, namely semi-structured interviews with 12 to 18 publishing executives, 24 to 36 firm-produced marketing documents, and 18 to 30 externally produced procurement and industry records, and it specified the population, inclusion criteria, purposeful sampling strategy, and justified sample size for each source (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022; Palinkas et al., 2015; Saunders et al., 2018). The chapter described the ethical protections of Institutional Review Board approval, informed consent, site permission, confidentiality, secure storage, and 3-year retention, and it presented the three researcher-developed instruments, together with their development and trustworthiness procedures (Bowen, 2009; Kallio et al., 2016). Data collection procedures were specified for each source, and the data analysis section established that the same six-phase thematic analysis will be applied to all three data sources within and across cases (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2021; Nowell et al., 2017). The chapter justified each assumption, distinguished and justified the delimitations, identified mitigation steps for each limitation, and addressed credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability individually (Berger, 2015; Birt et al., 2016; Lincoln & Guba, 1985). Chapter Four presents the results of applying these procedures.
